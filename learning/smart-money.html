What it is

Smart Money Concepts (SMC) is a trading vocabulary built on one claim: that large participants — funds, banks, institutional desks — cannot fill their orders without moving price, and that the shapes left behind when they do are recognisable on a chart. The names follow from that story. An order block is where the big order is supposed to have been placed. A liquidity sweep is price reaching past an obvious level to trigger other people's stops before reversing. A fair value gap is a price band the market skipped through too fast to trade properly, and is expected to revisit.

It is worth separating the story from the patterns. The patterns are shapes on a chart and can be defined, counted and tested. The story — that a specific institution placed a specific order at a specific candle — is not something any chart can confirm, and certainly not from the data this app has. This page keeps the two apart throughout: the measured section tests a pattern, and never claims to have identified who traded.

Figures on this page are generated from this app's own archive by scripts/build-learning-stats.js, not typed into the page.

The vocabulary

The core terms, with a straight answer on each about whether anything in this app detects it. Most of the answers are no — the point of the column is that the honest answer is visible next to the definition rather than buried below it.

TermWhat it meansDetected in this app?
Order block The last opposing candle before a strong directional move — read as the area where large orders were absorbed before the push. No. "Last opposing candle before a strong move" needs a threshold for "strong" that nobody agrees on. Nothing here implements it.
Fair value gap A three-bar sequence where bar 1 and bar 3 do not overlap, leaving a price band that never traded. Definable, and measured below. This is the one SMC pattern with no discretionary step, so it is the one this Hub tests.
Liquidity sweep Price pushes past an obvious high or low — where stop orders cluster — then closes back inside the range. Yes. The Super Model engine detects it. What it does with it afterwards is its own story.
Break of structure (BOS) Price closes beyond the prior swing point in the trend direction, said to confirm the trend continuing. No. Depends entirely on which swings you mark, which is a human choice — the same problem the Fibonacci guide runs into.
Change of character (CHoCH) The first break of structure against the prevailing trend, read as an early reversal warning. No. Inherits the swing-selection problem above.
Premium / discount The upper and lower halves of a marked range — sell in premium, buy in discount. No, though the chart's Fibonacci retracement tool draws the same 50% midpoint the idea rests on.
Mitigation block An order block price returns to so that trapped positions can exit at break-even. No. Requires knowing someone was trapped, which no daily bar records.

Footprint charts, and why there isn't one here

A footprint chart (also called order-flow or cluster charting) is a different thing from a candlestick chart. Instead of four numbers per bar, each bar is split into price levels, and every level shows how much volume traded at the bid versus at the ask. From that you get delta (ask volume minus bid volume), imbalance ratios between diagonal levels, and the absorption patterns traders watch for.

All of that needs one input: a record of every individual trade, tagged with whether it hit the bid or lifted the ask. That is tick data with quote context. It is not something you can reconstruct from a daily bar — a day's open, high, low, close and total volume are compatible with an unlimited number of different order flows.

There is no footprint chart in this app and there cannot be one from this data. Not "not yet" — the required inputs are absent, and no indicator setting recovers them. Anything labelled a footprint built from daily OHLCV would be an invention.

Fair value gaps, measured

Of everything in the vocabulary table, fair value gaps are the only pattern with a definition mechanical enough to count without a judgement call. So they are the one thing on this page that gets tested rather than described.

Rule A bullish gap exists when bar 3's low is above bar 1's high. A bearish gap is the mirror. The shaded band is the price range those two bars never shared.
Filter Gaps smaller than 1% of price are ignored. On thinly traded DSE names a sub-1% gap is usually just a tick-size artefact rather than a fast move.
Filled Counted as filled when a later bar trades back through the far edge of the band. Gaps too recent to have a full forward window are excluded, not counted as unfilled.
Example

The control column is the part that matters. "Gaps get filled most of the time" is not evidence of anything on its own — price wanders, and any level close enough to the current price gets touched eventually. So for every real gap, the same question was asked of a random non-gap bar in the same stock, using a level the same percentage away in the same direction. That is the control. The difference between the two columns is the only number here that says anything about gaps specifically.

What this does and does not show. Gaps are revisited more often than an equally distant arbitrary level — that difference is real and it is the direction SMC predicts. What it is not is a strategy result: there is no entry, no stop, no target and no position sizing anywhere in this measurement. A gap that fills after price first runs 20% the other way counts as "filled" here and would have been a painful trade. See Reading it honestly.

The one SMC signal actually coded here

routes/super-model-engine.js contains a liquidity sweep detector, and it is the only Smart Money Concept implemented anywhere in this codebase. The diagram below is drawn by that same rule, ported into this page — the highlighted bar is highlighted because the detector fired on it, not because it was drawn to look like it should.

Lookback 20 bars. The high and low of the previous 20 sessions define the range being swept.
Threshold 0.1%. The wick must exceed the range extreme by more than this, so a bar that merely equals the old high does not qualify.
Close Must come back inside the range. A bar that pushes through and holds is a breakout, not a sweep — the rule separates the two.
This bar
The Super Model detects this signal and deliberately scores it zero. Its weights come from scripts/compare-candidates.js, which tested candidate weight sets across four independent time windows. Liquidity sweep flipped sign depending on where the data was split — bullish in some windows, bearish in others — so no stable edge could be demonstrated and its weight was set to 0. The engine still surfaces it in the UI, tagged "Detected but not scored — this signal had no consistent edge in backtesting." That tag is not a placeholder; it is the finding.

Block trades — the closest thing to a real footprint

A block trade on the DSE is a large negotiated deal crossed off the continuous order book. If anything in this archive is a genuine institutional trace, it is this field — it is the only one that records size deliberately kept out of the normal market. The archive carries a daily count and quantity per stock.

Before reading anything into it, look at the coverage. The table below is every year in the archive, showing how many daily bars carry any block record at all.

Loading coverage…
2025 is empty. Not "low" — zero bars in the entire year carry a block-trade record, across every stock, while the same year has a full set of price bars. That is a data source gap, not a year in which the DSE recorded no block deals. The honesty section explains why this matters more than it might look: any study of block trades on this archive silently skips a year.
And even where it is populated, three things are missing: the price the block traded at, which side initiated it, and who the counterparty was. A block can be an institution accumulating, an institution exiting, a sponsor transferring shares to a family member, or a settlement of an off-market agreement. The field cannot tell those apart, so neither can any reading of it.

Reading it honestly

A fill rate is not a strategy return

The measured table says gaps are revisited more often than a matched arbitrary level. It does not say a trade based on that makes money. There is no entry price, no stop, no target and no cost assumption anywhere in it — and this app has already learned once, on MACD + EMA, that a real gross edge can be entirely consumed by commission. Treat the difference as an observation about price behaviour, not a signal.

The control is matched on distance, not on conditions

Each control bar is drawn at random from anywhere in the same stock's history, while real gaps cluster in volatile stretches — and price reaches any given level more easily when it is moving more. So part of the difference between the two columns is probably the volatility of the moment a gap forms rather than the gap itself. The comparison is far better than no control at all, and it is still not a clean one. Treat the difference as an upper bound on any gap-specific effect.

The naming implies knowledge the data does not contain

Calling a candle an "order block" asserts that an institution placed an order there. Nothing in a daily OHLCV row can support or refute that. The pattern may still be useful; the explanation attached to it is untested and, with this data, untestable. Those are two separate claims and only the first one is on the table.

Most of the vocabulary needs you to pick the swings first

Break of structure, change of character, premium/discount zones and mitigation blocks all start from a human marking which highs and lows count. Change the marking and the signals change. That is the same objection the Fibonacci guide raises, and it is why none of those terms are measured on this page — not because they are worthless, but because there is no single version of them to test.

The one SMC signal that was tested here failed to hold up

Liquidity sweep is coded, detected, displayed — and weighted zero, because its sign flipped across data splits. That is the strongest statement this codebase can make about any SMC idea, and it is a negative one. It applies to this market, this archive and this definition of a sweep; it is not a claim about order flow trading everywhere.

DSE market structure is not the market SMC was written for

Most SMC material is built around 24-hour foreign exchange and index futures — deep, continuous markets with no daily price limits. The DSE has circuit breakers, a fixed session, wide spreads on small caps and far less institutional participation. A gap here can be a limit move rather than an aggressive institutional fill, which is a different phenomenon wearing the same shape.

What would actually be needed to do this properly

Tick-level trade records with timestamps, trade prices and the prevailing bid/ask at each print. With those, delta, imbalance and absorption become computable and the claims on this page become testable. Without them, order-flow analysis on this archive is a vocabulary exercise, and the honest move is to say so rather than approximate it.
