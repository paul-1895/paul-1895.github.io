What it is

The Relative Strength Index, published by J. Welles Wilder in 1978, answers one narrow question: over the last N bars, what share of the total price movement was upward? If every bar closed higher, RSI is 100. If every bar closed lower, it is 0. If gains and losses were equal in total size, it is 50. Everything else people do with RSI — overbought lines, divergences, crossovers — is interpretation layered on top of that one ratio.

"Relative strength" here does not mean strength relative to other stocks. That is a different measure entirely (relative strength ranking, which the Minervini guide deals with). RSI compares a stock only to its own recent history. A stock at RSI 80 is not outperforming anything except its own past two weeks.

The formula

Three steps, and the whole argument on this page lives in step two.

StepWhat happensNote
1 · Split Each bar's change from the previous close is filed as a gain or a loss. A down day contributes 0 to gains, not a negative number. Uncontroversial — every implementation agrees here.
2 · Average Average the gains and average the losses over the lookback period. This is where implementations diverge. "Average" can mean a simple mean of the last N, or Wilder's forward-smoothed running average. They give different numbers forever.
3 · Normalise RS = avgGain / avgLoss, then RSI = 100 − 100/(1 + RS). Squashes any ratio into 0–100. When there are no losses at all the ratio is undefined, and code has to pick a fallback.
Wilder's smoothing, precisely. After seeding from the first N changes, each new average is (previous × (N − 1) + today) / N. Every bar in history keeps some weight forever, decaying but never dropping out. A simple N-bar mean does the opposite: a bar counts fully for N bars, then vanishes completely.

A discrepancy in this app

This app does not use Wilder's smoothing. candlestick_chart/indicators/rsi.js computes RSI from a plain simple average of the last 14 gains and losses — and routes/screener-technicals.js carries an identical copy, so the chart, the screener and the Super Model all inherit it.

WhereUsesConsequence
indicators/rsi.js Simple 14-bar mean of gains/losses The RSI pane drawn under every chart in this app.
screener-technicals.js The same function, copied Server-side scans, and the Hilega-Milega RSI(9).
TradingView, Investing.com, textbooks Wilder's forward smoothing Anyone comparing this app's RSI reading against another platform's is comparing two different indicators.
This is a real difference, not a rounding one. A simple mean makes RSI jump when a large move drops out of the back of the window — the price today did not change, but the indicator did. Wilder's version cannot do that, because nothing ever leaves the window abruptly. The next section measures how far apart the two actually get.

Which one is "right"? Wilder's is the standard, and it is what almost every other platform means by RSI — so if you are reading RSI levels from a book, a course or another chart, they are describing the dashed line, not this app's solid one. Neither is broken mathematically; they are two different indicators that share a name. The problem is only that the shared name hides it.

The levels, and what they actually mean

70 and 30 are conventions Wilder proposed, not thresholds derived from anything. The chart pane in this app shades above 70 red and below 30 green, and draws a line at 50. Here is what each level does and does not tell you.

LevelCommon readingThe catch
Above 70 "Overbought" — the move is stretched and due a pause. In a strong trend RSI can sit above 70 for weeks. Selling every touch of 70 in an uptrend means exiting the best stocks first. Wilder himself described high RSI as a sign of strength, not of a sell.
Below 30 "Oversold" — the fall is stretched and due a bounce. Equally, a stock in structural decline can print sub-30 readings all the way down. Oversold and cheap are unrelated concepts.
The 50 line The midpoint — above it, gains have outweighed losses over the window. The most defensible of the three, because it is a direct restatement of the arithmetic rather than a judgement about what is "too much". Hilega-Milega is built around it.
Period (14 vs 9) Shorter periods react faster. A shorter RSI also reaches the extremes far more often, so 70 on an RSI(9) is a much weaker statement than 70 on an RSI(14). The levels are not transferable between periods.

Divergence

The most popular advanced use of RSI: price makes a higher high while RSI makes a lower high (bearish divergence), or price makes a lower low while RSI makes a higher low (bullish divergence). The reasoning is that the second push happened with less momentum behind it.

Nothing in this app detects RSI divergence. Not the chart, not the screener, not the Super Model. Spotting one requires deciding which two peaks to compare, and that is a human choice with no rule behind it — the same objection the Fibonacci and Smart Money Concepts guides run into. Any divergence you see on a chart here, you found yourself, and no backtest on this Hub says anything about whether it works.

Two things worth knowing before relying on one. First, divergences are only obvious in hindsight — a lower RSI high becomes a divergence only once price has turned, and a trend can produce four of them in a row before the fifth one "works". Second, on this app's simple-mean RSI, the shape of a peak also depends on what dropped out of the back of the 14-bar window, which adds a source of wiggle that Wilder's version does not have.

What testing found

RSI on its own has never been backtested in this app — there is no rule attached to it to test. What has been tested is the one strategy built from it: the Hilega-Milega RSI(9)/WMA(21) crossover.

It did not survive. The Super Model measured its excess forward return across different splits of the history, the sign flipped depending on where the data was cut, and its weight in the shipped model is 0 — detected and displayed, but contributing nothing to any score. The full figures are on the Hilega-Milega page, which is where that result belongs rather than being restated here.

Note carefully what that does and does not implicate. It tests one crossover rule built on RSI(9), on this market, over this sample. It says nothing about RSI(14) as a descriptive measure, and nothing about the 70/30 levels, because neither has been given a testable rule here.

Reading it honestly

This app's RSI is not the RSI in your book

The single most useful thing on this page. If you read "buy below 30" somewhere and apply it to the pane in this app, you are applying a threshold calibrated on Wilder's smoothed version to a simple-mean version that reaches those extremes far more often. The measured section shows how much more often.

RSI can change without price changing

Because this app's version uses a fixed 14-bar window, a large gain or loss falling out of the back of that window shifts the reading on its own. A flat day can move RSI several points. That is an artefact of the windowing, not information about today.

Bounded does not mean mean-reverting

RSI cannot leave 0–100, and it is tempting to read that as "it must come back". The bound is arithmetic, not economic. A stock can hold RSI above 70 for a quarter while the price doubles, and the indicator will simply sit there.

The extremes are least reliable where they are most common

Thin DSE small caps can print an RSI of 100 or 0 on a handful of one-sided ticks, because with no losses in the window the ratio is undefined and the code falls back to an extreme. On an illiquid stock, an extreme RSI often measures the absence of trading rather than the presence of momentum.

No divergence detection exists here — including in the backtests

The most-cited RSI technique in trading education is entirely absent from this codebase, which also means no result on this Hub supports or undermines it. If you use divergences, you are outside anything this app has measured.

The one RSI rule that was tested here scored zero

Hilega-Milega is a mechanical, testable RSI strategy, it was tested properly, and it showed no consistent edge. That is one rule on one market — but it is the only evidence this app has, and it points down rather than up.
