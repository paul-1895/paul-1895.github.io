What it is

MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence) is the difference between two EMAs of price — a fast one and a slow one. When the fast EMA pulls away from the slow one, momentum is building; when they converge, it's fading. This app draws it with the standard 12/26/9 settings. "MACD + 50 EMA" is this app's own stricter variant: the plain crossover plus a 50-period EMA trend filter on price itself, so a signal only counts if the stock is already trending the right way.

MACD line EMA(12) of price minus EMA(26) of price. The fast line.
Signal line EMA(9) of the MACD line — a smoothing of a smoothing, same construction principle as Hilega-Milega's WMA(21) of RSI. The slow reference the fast line is measured against.
Histogram MACD line minus signal line, drawn as bars. Zero when they're equal — this is what actually flips sign at a crossover.

How it's computed

StepComputed fromConsequence
EMA(12), EMA(26) Closing price, two different lengths. Unlike this app's RSI-based indicators, these EMAs have no null-padded warm-up — they seed from the very first price and are simply less accurate on the first few dozen bars.
MACD line EMA(12) − EMA(26). Unbounded, unlike RSI — its scale depends entirely on the stock's own price level, which is why every diagram here is re-scaled per slice rather than fixed to 0–100.
Signal line The MACD line, EMA smoothed over 9 bars. Lags the MACD line by construction — exactly what makes a crossover a well-defined, testable event.
Two different "MACD" things this app reports. The Screener's plain "MACD Bullish / Bearish Cross" lists are just this crossover, on its own, on the daily / weekly / monthly timeframe. The stricter "MACD + 50 EMA — Buy / Exit" lists (this page's actual subject) additionally require the trend filter below, plus a stop/target bracket once in a trade. They are not the same signal, and — as the evidence section shows — they were tested separately with two different results.

The trend filter

A MACD crossover says nothing about the broader trend — it can fire during a downtrend's dead-cat bounce just as easily as at the start of a real move. The "+ 50 EMA" strategy filters that out: a bullish cross only counts as an entry if the crossover bar's close is above the 50-period EMA. Below it, the cross is still detected and shown — but rejected as a trade.

▍ Green shading marks stretches where price sits above its 50-EMA — the filter is open, new long entries are allowed. ▍ Red shading marks price below the EMA — any bullish cross here gets rejected, not actioned.

What this app actually detects

Two independent code paths, both real, both live in the Screener:

ListRuleSource
MACD Bullish / Bearish Cross Plain crossover on the most recently completed bar — daily, weekly, or monthly. No trend filter, no risk management. detectMacdCrossover() in routes/screener-technicals.js
MACD + 50 EMA — Buy / Exit Crossover bar closes above the 50-EMA (buy) or a bearish cross while a position is open (exit) — the strategy this whole page is about. routes/macd-strategy1.js, mirrored client-side in candlestick_chart/macd-backtest.js
The strategy is more than an entry rule. Once filled (at the next bar's open, to avoid look-ahead bias), it manages the position with a stop at the lowest low of the last 10 bars, a target at 2× that risk, and an exit on the next bearish cross — whichever comes first. Full rules are in the code comments at the top of candlestick_chart/macd-backtest.js.

What the backtest found

Unlike most signals on this app's Learning Hub, "MACD + 50 EMA" has already been replayed across the entire market — every stock, every historical signal, real stop/target/ commission accounting — not just sampled. The results are stored in data/macd-strategy1.json and power the MACD + 50 EMA page directly. This is the real output of that run:

MeasureResultMeaning
Stocks scanned396 392 had enough history to trade; 4 skipped.
Total trades14,951 ~38 trades per stock on average — this signal fires often.
Win rate31.32% 4,683 wins vs 10,263 losses. The 2:1 reward/risk target is meant to make a sub-50% win rate still profitable — see below for whether it did.
How trades closed 2,722 hit target · 1,515 stopped out · 10,714 exited on a reverse MACD cross Almost three-quarters of all trades ended on the reversal exit, not the stop or the target — winners rarely got to run to the 2R target.
Avg return per stock −13.49% Median was worse: −25.13% — more than half of all stocks lost over a quarter of their capital running this mechanically.
Avg buy & hold, same period +74.77% Simply holding the stock beat actively trading this strategy by roughly 88 percentage points on average.
Beat buy & hold158 of 392 stocks (~40%) Trading it actively lost to doing nothing on 3 out of 5 stocks.

This is not a cherry-picked bad run. It is every listed stock, every signal, the exact rules described above, commission included on both legs. Simple buy-and-hold beat it on 60% of stocks and by nearly 90 points on average.

Separately, the Super Model also tested the plain MACD crossover (no EMA filter, no risk management) as a candidate signal against forward returns. It measured an edge of +0.67 in the earlier half of its sample and −0.70 in the later half — the sign flipped, the signature of noise, not edge — and gave it a model weight of 0, the same treatment Hilega-Milega received. Two independent tests, two different methodologies, the same verdict: neither the raw crossover nor the fuller managed strategy has demonstrated a durable edge on this data.

Reading it honestly

The exit rule cuts winners short

10,714 of 14,951 trades — almost three in four — closed on a reverse MACD cross, not the 2R target. A strategy that lets losers run to a hard stop but exits winners on the first sign of momentum cooling has an asymmetry working against it, regardless of how good the entry timing is.

~38 trades a stock is not a low-cost strategy

At 0.4% commission per side, every round trip costs 0.8% before the trade even needs to be right. Multiplied across an average 38 trades per stock, that is real, compounding drag — a large part of why the average return sits so far below buy-and-hold even before counting the losing trades themselves.

It is one of the most widely taught strategies there is

MACD crossed against price above a long moving average is a textbook trend-following setup, not an obscure one. That popularity is exactly why it is worth testing rather than trusting — and on this data, mechanically executed, it lost to doing nothing on most stocks.
