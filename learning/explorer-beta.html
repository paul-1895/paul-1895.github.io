What it is

Explorer Beta is a complete, mechanical set of rules for buying DSE stocks and managing the position afterwards. It takes its candidates from the Super Model, refuses most of them through a series of gates, and for the few it accepts it prescribes an entry, a stop, a target, a ladder that raises the stop as the trade works, and the exact conditions under which it will exit. It buys only — there is no short mechanism on the DSE, so a sell side would be theoretical. It has its own page: Explorer Beta.

Why "Beta". The rules were chosen by measuring them on 2024–2026 price history and checking each one across four windows of that same history. That is in-sample selection: every rule has already seen the data it is scored on. The only genuine test is what happens to trades issued after the rules were fixed — the forward record — and at launch that record is empty. "Beta" is a promise not to claim more than that until the record exists.
Why "locked". The rule set was frozen on 2026-09-06 and the files that implement it are pinned by a test that fails if any of them changes. A forward record is only meaningful if the rules that produce it do not move: if a rule were tweaked each time a trade went badly, the live record would become the same kind of after-the-fact fit as the backtest. Improvements are therefore made to the Super Model's live Explorer variant, which keeps evolving as the research version. If a future rule set earns a launch, it becomes a new beta rather than a patch to this one.

Where the signal comes from

Explorer Beta does not have its own indicator. Its candidates are the stocks the Super Model's composite score rates as a buy with a conviction of 25 or more — the same threshold the rule set was measured with. The composite is a weighted sum of many detectors (moving-average structure, candlestick patterns, volume surges and the rest); the Super Model page and its guide explain how that score is built. Explorer Beta's own contribution begins after the score: it decides which of those candidates may actually be bought, and how each one is managed once it is.

A conviction score of 25 is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. On a typical day the gates below decline most of the candidates that reach them; on a weak-index day they can decline every one. The page reports how many were declined and by which gate, and it never relaxes a gate to produce a list.

The entry gates

Every one of these must pass on the signal day for a buy to be issued. Each row gives the rule and — from the notes recorded in the frozen plan when it was adopted — the one-line reason it is there. The chips are honest about which kind of reason that is: measured means the rule improved the backtest across the validation windows, preference means it was adopted as a risk or identity choice with its cost on record, and validity means it fixes something the simulation could not otherwise trust.

GateRuleWhy it is there
Candle closed green measured The signal day's own candle must close at or above its open. A same-day confirmation that cannot go stale, unlike the multi-day trend reads that were tried and rejected. Improved the buy average in all four validation windows — the cleanest sweep of any gate tested.
Volume above the prior day measured The signal day must trade more shares than the day before it. Participation behind the move rather than a signal firing into a quiet tape. A small improvement here, and notably one that hurt the shipped Super Model plan — an Explorer-specific adoption, not a market-wide law.
Upper wick ≤ 40% of range measured The signal candle's upper shadow may be at most 40% of the day's high-to-low range. A long upper wick means the rally was sold into, so "closed green" overstates what happened. Beat the ungated version in all four windows, with a monotonic threshold sweep — the signature of a real effect.
Momentum floor measured Price must already be up at least 20% over the prior 30 sessions. The opposite of what was first asked ("avoid extended entries"). Buys that were not yet moving averaged a loss; buys already up 20% or more averaged a gain, and the effect reproduced across several lookbacks. Flat entries are the illiquid ones whose signals fire off stale history. Cost: it declines roughly 70% of long entries.
Defended lows measured The signal candle's lower shadow must be at least 40% of the day's range. Sellers pushed price down and buyers took it back before the close. The opposite wick from the ceiling above: together they describe a bar that closed green, held its gains and was defended at its lows. The strongest single result measured on this model, though the mechanism is less cleanly understood than the momentum floor's.
Tighter wick when stretched preference measured If the entry sits more than 20% above its own 20-day simple moving average, the upper-wick ceiling tightens from 40% to 20%. A user directive with a rationale — an entry already far from its mean has less room to absorb a rejection. The trades it removes earned nothing in the backtest (none reached target), and neighbouring thresholds give the same result, so it is a plateau rather than a knife-edge.
No Bank-sector buys preference measured Stocks in the Bank sector are never bought. A momentum model holding the market's least-momentum sector: banks were the book's worst sector by total damage — big floats, slow tape, regulatory overhang. Won three of four windows. Combined with the merger exclusions below, Explorer Beta holds no banking exposure at all, by design.
Mutual Fund weekly rule measured For Mutual Funds only: the last completed week must have closed green and traded more than the week before. Fund units are NAV-anchored and thinly traded, so a momentum signal without weekly participation behind it is usually stale-history noise. The same weekly gate was rejected market-wide (it idles capital); for funds specifically it improved every measure. The sector was probed because it flagged, which is a post-hoc selection risk.
Dead-tape rule validity Refuse any signal on a stock whose close has been unchanged for ten sessions in a row. Floor-pinned, suspended or zero-liquidity stocks still produce signals off stale history, but they cannot actually move. A validity fix, not an edge claim: ten in a row is unambiguous, where a quiet penny stock can legitimately close flat for a few days.
DSEX index regime measured Buys are refused while the DSEX index's own 14-day RSI is below 45. A per-stock signal only says whether that stock is strong; it says nothing about the tape it trades on. Buy performance tracked index momentum monotonically — the weaker the index RSI, the worse the buy did — and the rule improved buy results in four of four windows with a clean dose-response.
Max-risk veto (30%) measured A trade whose honest, level-based stop would sit more than 30% below entry is refused outright. Trades of that shape risked a great deal to make a capped 12% and carried a large share of the dataset's losses. It rarely binds now that the 5% cap below pulls every stop in, but it stays as a guard in case that cap is ever lifted.
Excluded: FASFIN preference FAS Finance is never traded. A decade-long decline whose momentum signals fire on dead-cat bounces, on a share priced so low that a single tick is a double-digit percentage. No stop distance can hold there — the backtest recorded gap losses straight through the 5% cap.
Excluded: merger banks validity GIB, SIBL, EXIMBANK, FIRSTSBANK and UNIONBANK cannot be signalled on or after 2025/11/05. The five Shariah banks folded into the regulator-forced Sammilito Islami Bank merger. Their earlier history is real tape and stays in the backtest; the cut-off is the day trading froze, so no future run can signal a ticker that no longer trades on its own terms.
Two things the gates cannot see. A limit-up day with no range at all (high equals low) has no measurable wick, so it passes every wick rule by default — a known gap the plan notes but does not fix. And the momentum floor's cost is opportunity, not accuracy: it declines most of the entries the Super Model generates, which is acceptable only because the model produces far more signals than a fixed amount of capital can hold.

Managing the trade

Once a buy is issued the rules below take over. They are the same rules the page prints under each suggestion's "Rules" heading, in the order they matter. The stop only ever moves in the trade's favour; nothing here can loosen it.

RuleWhat it doesWhy, and the cost
5% stop cap preference The protective stop is placed at the nearest honest support below entry, then pulled in so that it is never more than 5% away. A user directive — "no stop-loss should be greater than 5%" — adopted with its cost on record: it lowered the backtested average and win rate, because a 5% stop sits inside ordinary DSE daily noise, while it reduced the spread of outcomes. A risk-preference choice, not an edge improvement.
Close-confirmed stop preference measured The stop books only when a daily close finishes below it, and the exit price is that close — not the stop price. A dip through it during the day that recovers by the close is ignored. Also a user directive, and the strongest validated change in this rule set's history — better in all four windows and at every portfolio size. The documented price: when a day does close through the stop, the loss books deeper than 5%, because the fill is wherever that close lands. The 5% figure now bounds where the stop sits, not what a losing exit costs.
Target caps: 1:2.5 and 12% preference The target is the tighter of 2.5 times the stop distance and 12% above entry. An intrabar touch of the target takes profit there, and it outranks a close-confirmed stop on the same day. With a 5% stop the 1:2.5 cap gives 12.5%, so in practice the 12% cap almost always binds. These caps are stated on the page as a risk preference rather than measured edge: they bound every winner, which is also why the blended result stays modest regardless of entry quality.
Profit-lock ladder measured As the day's high reaches each trigger, the stop rises to the paired lock. The rungs are listed in the next section. Locks in an increasing floor of profit as the move extends. The rungs arm off the intrabar high — this is how profits are booked, and it keeps that mechanic even though the losing stop is close-confirmed.
S/R rungs measured Crossing each resistance level between entry and target also locks the stop at that level. A level price has already cleared is a natural place to defend; these rungs sit alongside the percentage rungs and whichever is higher wins.
1% lock fill floor measured A lock only closes the trade if the price it can actually fill at is at least 1% in profit. Otherwise it stands aside and the ordinary protective stop governs. Rungs arm off the high, fills are taken at the next open, and settlement can freeze the shares in between — so a rung could arm on a wick that was never sellable and then execute far below it, booking a loss under the name "profit lock". The MBL1STMF case below is the worked example. Improved all four windows and every portfolio size, with the same trades simply exiting better.
Support-break exit measured A daily close below the nearest support between entry and the stop exits at that close, even though the stop was not reached. Role-reversed levels count: a resistance that price broke through on the way up is watched as support. A risk-control trade-off: slightly lower average return, bought with a materially smaller average losing trade and a narrower spread. Adding the role-reversed levels shrank the average loss again and resolved trades faster, freeing the one-trade-per-stock slot sooner.
60-session holding window validity If none of the above has fired within 60 trading days, the plan books an exit at that day's close. The window every Explorer backtest used. A trade that has gone nowhere for three months is not the trade the entry gates described, and the slot is worth more to the next candidate.
Order of precedence on a single day. A target touch wins over everything. Then a binding profit lock touched during the day exits at its fill (subject to the 1% floor). Then, at the close, the protective stop and the support-break level are checked — both book only on the close. Nothing can execute while the shares are still settling.

The profit-lock ladder

Nine rungs. Each is a pair: once the day's high is at least the trigger distance above entry, the stop moves to the lock distance above entry. Note the first step: reaching +2.1% locks +2%, and the next rung is not +3 but +4. From there the rungs run one percent apart up to +11%, just under the 12% target.

On top of these, every resistance level that sits between entry and target becomes an extra rung: when price crosses it, the stop locks at that level. The ladder and the S/R rungs are combined by taking whichever stop is higher, and the stop never moves back down.

The 1% fill floor, and the case that produced it. On MBL1STMF, entered on 2026/07/12, the +2% rung armed on the next day's high of ৳4.70. But the shares were still settling and could not be sold until 07/14, and that day opened at ৳4.50 — so the "profit lock" would have filled at a loss of about 2.2%. With the floor in place the lock declines that fill, the ৳4.37 hard stop is never threatened, and the trade goes on to exit two days later at ৳4.784 for a gain of 4%. The floor exists so that a rule named "profit lock" can never book a loss.

Settlement: T+2 and T+3

On the DSE, shares bought on day T are not in your account — and cannot be sold — until T+2 for A, B and N category stocks, or T+3 for Z category. Explorer Beta's simulation enforces this, and so does the live guidance: no exit of any kind can execute before the shares settle. A stop breached while the position is frozen books on the first sellable day it still stands breached, at that day's price.

Why it matters more than it sounds. Two of the rules above exist because of settlement. The 1% lock floor was written after a rung armed during the freeze and would have filled far below its level. And the backtest's single worst trade in eleven years of history is a gap that opened during a settlement freeze — a loss no stop rule could have prevented, because nothing could be sold. An earlier version of the simulation ignored settlement and looked considerably better; that version's results are no longer trusted.

Open trades Your own open positions from the Trades page, each managed under Explorer Beta's rules from your entry price and date, with a live quote, an action label, the current stop, lock, target and support-break levels, and a candle read. Grouped into "Managed", "Plan-exited" and "Outside the rules" (short positions, unknown dates, or no price history). The action labels are explained in the next section.
Candle reads Two readings per trade: the patterns detected on the last completed session's bar, and — only while quotes are live — a provisional read of today's still-forming bar. The live bar's open is approximated by yesterday's close, because the quote source does not carry today's true open. Treat the live read as a sketch that can change by the close; the completed-bar read is the reliable one.
Forward record Every suggestion Explorer Beta has issued since launch, resolved through the frozen rules on every read: open, target, stop, locked stop, or expired, with the running win rate and average. This is the only out-of-sample evidence there is. It starts empty and is not worth interpreting until it holds a meaningful number of resolved trades. Nothing about the live rules can be said before then.
Launch backtest The two-year Explorer backtest the beta launched on: trade count, net win rate, net average per trade, target and stop rates, spread, average bars held, period. Served live from the backtest file, with its caveats printed under it. The page's own words: read these numbers as an upper bound — the ceiling, not the expectation.

The action labels

Each managed open trade carries one label. It is the plan's instruction for today, worked out from the current quote, the stop after every rung the tape has armed so far, and whether the shares have settled.

LabelMeaningWhat the plan does
Frozen · settlement The shares have not settled yet (fewer than T+2 or T+3 completed sessions). Nothing can be sold. The plan keeps tracking the tape; if the stop is breached in the meantime it books on the first sellable day it still stands breached.
Hold Price is inside the plan: above the current stop, below the target, and not below the support-break level. Do nothing. The card shows how far the stop and target sit from the current price, and whether a profit lock is already binding.
Take profit Today's high has touched the target. The plan sells at the target. A target touch outranks a close-confirmed stop on the same day.
Lock hit — sell A binding profit lock has been touched today, and the fill available is at least 1% in profit. The plan books the exit at the lock. If the available fill were under the 1% floor the label would stay at Hold, with a note explaining that the lock stood aside and the protective stop governs.
Stop if close below Price is currently trading below the protective stop. Not an exit yet. If today's close finishes below the stop the plan exits at that close; if price recovers above the stop by the close, nothing happens.
Support break if close Price is above the stop but below the nearest support the plan watches. Same close-confirmation logic: a close below that support exits at the close even though the stop was never reached.
Plan-exited (no instruction) Replaying your trade from its entry date, the plan would already have closed it — at a target, a stop, or the end of the holding window — on a date the card shows. The position is now outside the plan and carries no further instruction. The card tells you when and at what price the plan would have exited, and leaves the decision to you.
Two of these labels — "Stop if close below" and "Support break if close" — are conditional. They describe what will happen if the day closes where price is now, not something to act on the moment they appear. That is the close-confirmed rule doing exactly what it was adopted to do: refusing to be shaken out by an intraday dip.

Caveats

These are the standing caveats recorded alongside the rule set itself, restated in plain language. They apply to every number on the Explorer Beta page.

The rules were chosen in-sample. Every gate and every knob was selected by measuring it on 2024–2026 price data, then validated on windows drawn from that same history. The backtest is therefore the best the rules could look on the data that chose them. The forward record is the first out-of-sample test, and it did not exist at launch.
Several rules are preferences, not measured edge. The 5% stop cap lowered the backtested average and win rate. The close-confirmed stop exchanged a bounded same-day loss for fewer but deeper losses. The target caps bound every winner. The Bank exclusion and the tighter wick rule for stretched entries began as directives, even where the measurement later agreed. The plan records each of these as a choice, with its cost.
The returns are concentrated. A large share of the backtested gain comes from a handful of thinly traded non-bank financial names. The simulation assumes fills at those prices; a real order in a thin book may not get them, and a few good trades in a few names is a fragile foundation.
No forward record at launch. Until the tracked record holds enough resolved trades to compare against the backtest, nothing can be said about how the rules behave on unseen data — good or bad.
No shorting on the DSE. The rule set is buy-only. The backtest's blended figure includes a theoretical sell book that cannot be traded; ignore it in favour of the buy figures, which are the ones the page shows.
Not financial advice. Explorer Beta is a research tool that applies a fixed set of rules to price data. It does not know your circumstances, your costs or your risk tolerance, and its guidance for your open trades is a mechanical replay of those rules from your entry — not a recommendation.

Reading it honestly

Locking the rules is the point, not a limitation

It is tempting to read "frozen" as "cannot improve". The value of Explorer Beta is precisely that its record cannot be edited after the fact. Every improvement anyone thinks of goes to the live research variant, where it can be measured without contaminating this record.

Four-of-four is the bar, and it is a low one

"Improved in all four validation windows" sounds decisive, but the windows overlap in regime and are drawn from the same two years. Several rules that once passed that test were later found to fail when re-measured against a changed baseline — the plan's own notes record two such reversals. The bar filters noise; it does not prove edge.

Most of what it does is refuse

The momentum floor alone declines about seven in ten candidates. A rule set this selective can only be judged with capital in mind: a gate that lifts the average per trade while leaving the account idle for half the year is a worse gate, which is why two strong-looking filters (an index trend gate and a market-wide weekly volume gate) were measured and left out.

The "why" column is the record, not a guarantee

The reasons in the gates table are the reasons recorded when each rule was adopted. They are honest about what was measured and what was chosen, but a measured effect on 2024–2026 data is a description of that period, not a law of the market.
