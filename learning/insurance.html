The sector

Insurance is one of the most heavily populated corners of the DSE — the exchange lists dozens of insurers, far more than most comparable markets carry, and almost all of them are small. The DSE classifies them into two separate sectors: Insurance, meaning general (non-life) insurers, and Life Insurance. This guide covers both, because they share a regulator and a listing history but almost nothing else about how they make money.

The sector is supervised by the Insurance Development and Regulatory Authority (IDRA), which sets solvency, expense and product rules. The background fact worth carrying into everything below is that insurance penetration in Bangladesh is very low by international standards — a small share of national income is spent on premiums. That cuts two ways: it is the entire growth case for the sector, and it is also why so many listed insurers are competing hard for a small pool of business.

What this page is. A description of how these businesses work, plus real measurements of how their shares have traded, computed from this app's own daily price archive. It is not a valuation of any of them: the archive holds prices, not premiums, claims, reserves or solvency margins. Where the data cannot support a claim, the page says so instead of estimating.

How insurers actually earn

An insurer has two separate profit engines, and confusing them is the most common mistake in reading one. Understanding the split explains why insurance shares often move with interest rates rather than with anything insurance-specific.

EngineWhat it isWhat decides whether it works
Underwriting result Premiums earned, minus claims paid, minus the cost of acquiring and administering the business. Pricing discipline and claims experience. In a crowded market with many similar competitors, price competition and distribution commissions are what erode this line.
Investment income Return on the float — premiums collected now against claims paid later, held in deposits, bonds, and equities meanwhile. Prevailing interest rates, above all. A rise in deposit rates lifts this line across the whole sector at once, regardless of how any individual insurer is underwriting.
Reinsurance Passing on part of large risks to a reinsurer in exchange for part of the premium. Caps the damage from a single catastrophic claim, at the cost of ceding premium. Terms tighten after bad loss years.
Why this matters for reading the tables below. A sector-wide move in insurance share prices is at least as likely to be about the rate environment, or about a regulatory change affecting everyone, as about any insurer's own underwriting. Nothing in this app can separate those, because none of the inputs are in the archive.

General versus life — two different businesses

General (non-life)Life
Typical products Fire, marine cargo, motor, engineering, miscellaneous. Endowment and term policies, often long-dated, sold to individuals.
Policy length Usually one year, repriced at renewal. Many years — the liability is committed long before it is paid.
Main volume driver Trade and economic activity. Marine cargo cover in particular follows import volumes and letters of credit, so it moves with the import cycle. Household saving, agent recruitment, and public trust in whether claims get paid.
Characteristic risk A bad loss year — a large fire or marine claim can erase a small insurer's annual profit. Lapses and surrenders. A policy abandoned early can leave the insurer having paid the acquisition cost without collecting the premiums that were meant to recover it.
Reputation sensitivity Moderate — buyers are often businesses meeting a contractual requirement. High. Life insurance is sold on the promise of a payment decades away, and publicised claim-settlement failures at individual companies affect willingness to buy across the sector.

The measured tables below keep the two sectors separate for exactly this reason. As you will see, they have not behaved alike.

What moves the sector

DriverMechanismVisible in this app?
Interest rates Higher deposit and bond yields lift investment income on the float across every insurer simultaneously. No. No rate series is stored anywhere in this repository.
Import volumes Marine cargo premiums follow trade finance activity, so an import squeeze feeds through to general insurers' top line. No. No trade data here.
Regulatory change Expense and commission limits, solvency rules, capital requirements and distribution reforms all reshape sector economics at once. No. Only as an unexplained common move in the price series.
New distribution channels Selling insurance through bank branches reaches customers agents cannot, and changes who captures the commission. No. Premium mix is not in the archive.
Catastrophe losses Floods, cyclones and major fires hit general insurers' claims in concentrated bursts. No. Claims are not in the archive.
Speculative flows Small free floats and low prices make several of these shares easy to move, independent of any of the above. Partly. Turnover and volatility in the tables above are the closest available proxy.
Five of the six drivers are invisible to this app. That is the honest summary of what a price archive can do. The tables measure how the shares traded; they do not explain why, and any explanation offered for a sector-wide move on this page would be a story rather than a finding.

Reading it honestly

A strong one-year median is not evidence of a strong sector

When a large group of small stocks all rise together over a year while their three- and five-year medians remain far weaker, the most economical description is a re-rating episode rather than a change in the underlying businesses. This app cannot confirm or refute that — it has no premium, claims or reserve data — but it is worth holding the possibility in mind before reading a good year as a trend.

Returns here are price only

No dividend is added back anywhere in these tables. Insurers are regular cash payers, so their real total return to a holder has been higher than the price column shows. The dividend column tells you the most recent declaration, not what reinvesting it would have earned.

The median hides the range

A sector median says what the middle stock did. In a group this size, the best and worst performers over the same year can be tens of percentage points apart — the constituent table is there precisely so the spread is visible rather than averaged away.

Only currently listed companies are in here

The archive is built from tickers that exist today. Anything delisted along the way is simply absent, which biases every long-horizon figure upward by an amount this app cannot measure. Treat the five-year column as the survivors' five years.

A short history can mean several different things

Where a stock's history starts late, the cause may be a recent listing, a ticker change, or a gap in the price source — and this app cannot tell them apart. That is why missing three- and five-year cells are left blank rather than filled from whatever history happens to exist.

Category is a governance signal, not a quality score

The A/B/N/Z categories reflect whether a company held its AGM and declared a dividend, as classified by the exchange. Z is a genuine red flag about corporate behaviour. But an A category is a low bar cleared, not a statement that the business is sound, and nothing here assesses solvency — which for an insurer is the measure that matters most.
