The sector

Textile is the largest sector on the DSE by number of listed companies. It is also the one most likely to be misread, because the industry it is named after — ready-made garments — is the backbone of Bangladesh's export economy, and the connection between that national story and these particular shares is much weaker than it first appears.

Ready-made garments dominate Bangladesh's merchandise exports and employ millions of people, overwhelmingly women. That much is uncontroversial and well documented. What follows from it for an investor is not, and the next section is the single most useful thing on this page.

What this page is. Real measurements of how these shares have traded, computed from this app's own daily price archive, plus a description of the industry behind them. It contains no earnings, order-book or capacity data, because none of that exists in this repository.

The listing gap

Most of Bangladesh's large garment exporters are privately held. They have not needed public equity — the industry has historically been financed by bank credit and retained earnings, and family ownership is the norm. The result is that the DSE's Textile sector is not a way to own the country's export champions.

What people assumeWhat is actually listed
Large export-oriented garment manufacturers selling to global brands. A minority of the sector. Some genuine exporters are listed, but they are not the household names of the industry.
Direct exposure to national garment export growth. Mostly backward-linkage businesses — spinning mills, weaving, dyeing and finishing — which sell yarn and fabric to the exporters rather than exporting themselves.
Companies whose fortunes track apparel demand in the US and EU. Companies whose margins track cotton and yarn prices, energy supply, and how promptly their domestic customers pay.
The practical consequence. "Exports are strong, so buy textile stocks" does not follow. A spinning mill can be squeezed by exactly the conditions that make its customers' exports competitive — cheap imported fabric, for instance, is good for a garment maker and bad for a domestic mill. The listed sector and the national industry are related but not the same trade.

Where the money is made along the chain

StageWhat it doesMargin character
Spinning Turns raw cotton into yarn. Capital-intensive, energy-hungry, continuous process. A spread business: yarn price minus cotton price minus energy. Both ends move independently, so margins can invert quickly. Heavy fixed costs make it painful to run below capacity.
Weaving / knitting Turns yarn into fabric. Competes directly with imported fabric, so pricing power depends partly on trade policy and partly on lead times.
Dyeing & finishing Colours and treats fabric to a buyer's specification. The most energy- and water-intensive stage, and the most exposed to environmental compliance costs and gas supply.
Garment manufacture Cuts and sews fabric into finished apparel for export. Labour-intensive rather than capital-intensive. Margins are thin and set by buyers, but the business scales without the fixed-cost trap of a mill.
Energy is the recurring constraint. Spinning, weaving and dyeing all need reliable gas and electricity, and interruptions or tariff increases hit these businesses directly through both output and unit cost. It is the one input that shows up across nearly every stage of the chain at once.

What moves it

DriverMechanismVisible in this app?
Cotton and yarn prices The input and output of a spinning mill. The gap between them, not either level, is what determines the margin. No. No commodity prices are stored here.
Energy supply and tariffs Gas and electricity availability set how much of installed capacity can actually run; tariffs set what it costs. No.
Taka exchange rate Cuts both ways: helps exporters' taka revenue, raises the cost of imported cotton, machinery and dyes. No. Net effect differs by company and cannot be inferred from prices.
Buyer demand in the US and EU Order volumes and prices from global apparel brands flow down the chain with a lag. No.
Working capital and bank credit These are receivables-heavy businesses. Tight credit or slow-paying customers can stress a mill that is otherwise operating normally. No. Balance sheets are not in the archive.
Speculative activity in low-priced shares Many of these shares trade at low absolute prices on small floats, which makes them easy to move regardless of operating performance. Partly. The volatility and turnover columns are the available proxy.

Reading it honestly

Check the Z count before anything else on this page

Z category means the company failed to hold an AGM or declare a dividend, as classified by the exchange. A large Z share within a sector is a statement about how many of its companies are not currently rewarding shareholders at all — and it is worth comparing the Z and non-Z performance columns in the constituent table before drawing any conclusion about the sector as a whole.

Liquidity is the constraint the return columns don't show

A median daily turnover figure well below the market's means the typical stock here trades thinly. A return you can see in a table is not the same as a return you could have captured: entering or exiting size in a thin name moves its price against you, and nothing in these columns accounts for that.

A big one-year number after a weak five-year number is a specific shape

When a sector's one-year median is strongly positive while its three- and five-year medians remain deeply negative, what is being described is a bounce inside a longer decline, not a recovery to previous levels. Both facts are in the table; reading only the first column inverts the picture.

"Textile" is one label over several different businesses

The sector classification comes from the exchange's own list. It puts spinners, weavers, dyers and garment makers in one bucket even though a cotton price move helps some and hurts others. A sector median across businesses that respond oppositely to the same input is a weak summary by construction.

Only survivors are counted

The archive holds tickers that exist today. Companies that were delisted are absent entirely, which flatters every long-horizon median by an amount this app cannot quantify. In a sector with this many distressed companies, that bias is likely to be larger than elsewhere.

Nothing here is fundamental analysis

There is no capacity utilisation, no order book, no cotton hedging position and no debt figure anywhere in these tables, because none of it is in the price archive. What is measured is how the shares traded. Anyone deciding on these companies needs the accounts, and this page is not a substitute for them.
