The sector

Paper & Printing is one of the smallest sectors on the DSE — a handful of companies making paper, board and packaging materials, plus printing and converting operations. Their end customers are mostly domestic: packaging for consumer goods and pharmaceuticals, paper for education and government use, and printing for commercial buyers.

As an industry it is unglamorous and quite stable in its demand drivers — packaging consumption grows roughly with the consumer economy, and paper for the education system is a large, predictable annual requirement. As a group of listed shares it behaves nothing like that description, which is the most interesting thing about it and the subject of the section below the tables.

What this page is. A description of the industry plus real measurements of how these six shares have traded, computed from this app's own daily price archive. It holds no production, cost or earnings data, because none of that exists in this repository.

How the business works

ElementWhat it meansConsequence
Inputs Wood pulp, waste paper for recycling, chemicals — much of it imported. Input costs are set in international markets and paid in foreign currency, while output is sold domestically in taka. A currency move squeezes the middle.
Energy intensity Pulping, drying and finishing are heavy continuous consumers of gas and electricity. Energy supply interruptions and tariff increases hit throughput and unit cost at the same time — the same constraint the textile mills face.
Capital intensity A paper machine is a large fixed asset that wants to run continuously. High operating leverage: profit swings far more than volume does, in both directions.
Import competition Finished paper and board can be imported, so domestic pricing is capped by landed import cost. Pricing power depends partly on trade policy and freight, not only on domestic demand.
Packaging demand Driven by consumer goods, pharmaceuticals and e-commerce volumes. The most structurally growing part of the sector, and the reason it is not simply a declining "paper" business.

What the numbers suggest — and what they don't prove

Three features of the table above are worth putting side by side, because individually they mean little and together they describe a specific pattern.

ObservationOrdinary explanationAlternative explanation
Very high volatility Small companies with high operating leverage genuinely have volatile earnings. Small free floats amplify any buying or selling into large price moves.
Turnover well above what the sector's size implies Genuine investor interest in a growing packaging market. Concentrated speculative activity — heavy trading in a few small names, not broad participation.
Wide dispersion between constituents Companies at different points in their capital cycles perform differently. Individual names moving on stock-specific activity rather than on anything sector-wide.
This app cannot choose between those columns. Distinguishing genuine interest from concentrated activity needs order-level data — who traded, in what size, against whom — and this archive has daily bars only. The Smart Money Concepts guide sets out exactly which fields exist and which do not. Both explanations are listed because both remain open, and presenting either as the finding would overstate what a price series can establish.

What moves it

Pulp and waste paper prices The dominant raw material cost, set internationally. No. No commodity data here.
Taka exchange rate Inputs are largely imported and output is sold domestically, so a weaker taka compresses margins directly. No.
Energy supply and tariffs Determines how much capacity can run, and at what cost. No.
Packaging demand Follows consumer goods, pharmaceutical and e-commerce volumes. No.
Government and education paper orders Large, lumpy annual procurement that can dominate a smaller producer's year. No.
Stock-specific trading activity In a sector this small with floats this thin, activity in one name can set the sector's headline figures. Partly. Turnover and volatility are the proxy — they show that something is happening, never what.

Reading it honestly

Six companies cannot support a sector conclusion

With this few constituents, the median is close to an arbitrary pick among them and any sector-level statement is really a statement about two or three shares. The constituent table is the substance of this page; the summary row is a convenience.

High turnover in a small sector is not the same as liquidity

A high median daily turnover here comes from heavy trading in a small number of names. That is not the same as being able to buy or sell size without moving the price — and it can reverse abruptly, since activity that arrived quickly can leave the same way.

Z category matters more in a sector this concentrated

One Z-category company among forty is a footnote. The same proportion among six is a significant share of the sector not currently rewarding shareholders, and it should be read directly off the constituent table rather than through the sector average.

Several of these histories are short

Where a ticker's archive begins recently, the cause may be a recent listing, a symbol change, or a gap in the price source — this app cannot tell which. Blank three- and five-year cells mean "not measurable here", never "zero", and in a six-company sector a couple of blanks materially thin the long-horizon comparison.

Returns exclude dividends

Every return column is price only. For the companies here that do pay, the figures understate what a holder actually received; for those that do not, the price line is the whole story. The dividend column is the only place that distinction appears.

Nothing here is a view on any of these companies

This page describes an industry and measures how six shares traded. It contains no production volumes, no input costs, no debt and no margins, because the price archive holds none of them. Anyone acting on these companies needs the accounts.
