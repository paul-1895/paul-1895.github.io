The sector

Telecommunication is the smallest sector on the DSE by company count and one of the largest by market weight. It contains three listed companies: two mobile network operators and one wholesale bandwidth provider. Everything about how this sector behaves follows from that shape — a handful of very large, heavily traded, foreign-affiliated businesses in a market where most sectors consist of dozens of small ones.

Bangladesh's mobile market is a small number of operators serving a very large subscriber base, regulated by the Bangladesh Telecommunication Regulatory Commission (BTRC). The regulator controls spectrum allocation, tariff floors, licence conditions and — through a significant-market-power framework — what the largest operator is permitted to do competitively. Sector economics here are set in a regulator's office to an unusual degree.

Not every operator is listed. The DSE carries two of the country's mobile operators; others are not publicly traded here. So this sector is a partial view of the industry, and market-share shifts toward an unlisted competitor are invisible in these three price series.

The three companies

TickerBusinessWhat it depends on
GP Grameenphone — the largest mobile operator, majority-controlled by Telenor Group. Subscriber numbers, average revenue per user, spectrum costs, and the regulatory conditions attached to being the dominant operator.
ROBI Robi Axiata — mobile operator majority-owned by Malaysia's Axiata Group, with Bharti as the other large shareholder, running the Robi and Airtel brands. The same drivers, from a smaller base — which makes subscriber and data growth matter proportionally more, and scale economics matter less favourably.
BSCPLC Bangladesh Submarine Cables — wholesale international bandwidth via submarine cable landing infrastructure. A different business entirely: it sells capacity to internet providers and operators rather than to consumers. Its economics are infrastructure-like, and it competes with terrestrial cross-border routes.
A three-stock median is not a sector statistic. Every "median" for this sector in the tables below is the middle of three numbers. It carries none of the averaging that makes a median meaningful in a sector of forty companies, and a single company's move can become the sector's headline figure. How a mobile operator earns

ElementWhat it meansWhy it matters here
ARPU Average revenue per user — total service revenue divided by subscribers. In a market where nearly everyone already has a connection, growth has to come from ARPU rather than from adding people. That makes data usage and pricing the whole game.
Subscriber churn The share of customers leaving each period. Low switching costs and easy SIM replacement make churn a permanent operating cost rather than an occasional problem.
Spectrum and licence costs Large, lumpy payments to the regulator for the right to operate on particular frequencies. These land as big capital outflows in specific years and can reshape a year's cash flow and dividend capacity on their own.
Network capital expenditure Continuous spending on towers, transmission and each generation of network technology. The reason telecom operators can report solid operating profits and still have constrained free cash flow.
Taxes and levies The sector carries a comparatively heavy tax and duty burden, including charges applied at the point of customer usage. Changes here move sector economics without any change in operating performance — and they are decided in a national budget, not by the companies.
Dividend policy Mature operators with limited growth outlets tend to return a high share of earnings as cash. A large part of the total return from these shares has historically come as dividends — which the price-only columns below do not capture.

What moves it

DriverMechanismVisible in this app?
Regulatory decisions Spectrum pricing, tariff floors, licence renewals and market-power rulings directly set what operators may charge and must pay. No. Only as an unexplained move in the price series.
Taxes and duties on usage Changes in sector-specific levies feed straight into effective prices for customers and into operator margins. No.
Data demand growth Rising per-user data consumption is the main route to revenue growth in a saturated subscriber market. No. No subscriber or usage data here.
Network investment cycles Each technology generation requires a capital programme before it earns anything. No.
Foreign parent decisions Both operators are majority foreign-owned; group-level strategy, repatriation and stake changes affect the listed entity. No. Shareholding structure is not in the archive.
Index and institutional flows Large weights make these shares a default holding for index-aware and institutional portfolios, so they move on flows unrelated to operations. Partly. The turnover column shows the scale of activity, not who is behind it.

Reading it honestly

Three companies is not a sample

Every statistical convention on this Hub — medians, dispersion, comparison against a market baseline — assumes enough constituents for the middle to mean something. With three, the sector median is just the middle company. Treat the sector row in the ranking table as a label for these three names, not as a measurement of an industry.

Low volatility is a description, not a safety rating

Large, liquid, institutionally held shares move less day to day than small speculative ones. That is a fact about trading behaviour. It says nothing about regulatory risk, competitive risk or the risk of a large one-off levy — all of which arrive as step changes rather than as daily volatility, and none of which this app can see coming.

Price-only returns understate this sector more than most

These are high-payout businesses. Excluding dividends from a return calculation is a general limitation of these tables, but its size here is unusually large, and it is concentrated in exactly the sector where holders have historically been paid to wait.

One of the three isn't a mobile operator

Grouping a wholesale bandwidth infrastructure business with two consumer mobile operators is the exchange's classification, not an economic judgement. Their revenue models, customers and competitive pressures differ substantially, which is another reason a three-company median is a weak summary.

Short price history can mean a ticker change rather than a new listing

Where the archive's history for a ticker starts recently, the cause may be a renamed symbol, a recent listing, or a gap in the source feed — and this app cannot distinguish them. That is why three- and five-year cells are left blank rather than computed from whatever history is present.

Nothing here values these companies

No subscriber count, no ARPU, no capital expenditure figure and no spectrum liability appears anywhere in this app's price archive. The tables measure how three shares traded. Any judgement about what they are worth needs the accounts and the regulatory filings, neither of which is here.
